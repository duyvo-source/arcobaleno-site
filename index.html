Reproducibility

The backtest its own author could not reproduce

Published result
−1.3191%
Mean net return per period, reported as the strategy's performance.
→
What it actually was
One draw
A sample from a distribution that changed on every process launch.

The ranking step sorted with sorted(signals.keys(), key=…), where signals was built by iterating a set. Python's sort is stable, so ties broke on set iteration order — randomised per process by PYTHONHASHSEED.

Ties were not exotic. Roughly 55% of this venue's funding settlements sit exactly at the clamped interest baseline, so any asset pinned there across a 168-hour window had an identical trailing mean. Different assets entered the portfolio on different runs.

Why it matters The reproduction gate was unmeetable by the original author re-running their own code, and no care in reimplementation closes it. Every downstream decision — deploy, size, shelve — rested on a number that would not come back. The fix was one line: sort by (signal, asset).

Data integrity

One corrupt day, thirty corrupt months

Reported by the data feed
172,635%
A single day's base APY for one liquidity pool.
→
That pool's median
0.91%
The value the same feed reported on every other day.

That one day contaminated 30 rolling 30-day windows and produced a pool-level result of +192.66%/yr on a WBTC–WETH pool — two highly correlated majors, where that return is structurally impossible.

It passed everything. The validation checks all operated on windows, and the corruption entered before the windows were formed. The result looked like an outlier worth investigating, not like bad data.

The research already had the right check — implied fee tier against published fee tier — and had run it on two pools, on one day. That is a spot check. Applied across the panel, it catches the corruption immediately.

Why it matters The difference between a spot check and a guard is whether it runs on every row. A value that is obviously impossible to a human can be completely invisible to code, because the code was never asked.

Cost model

Three strategies closed for a reason that wasn't true

Cost as charged
≈2.0% / period
Summed across positions while P&L was averaged across them.
→
Cost as incurred
0.10% / period
Overstated by a factor of 19–20 — the position count.

Cost per unit notional therefore rose as a fixed book was split across more names, which cannot happen under correct book-level accounting: more names at constant gross means smaller positions. Verified across all 590 periods of three backtest ledgers against the identity net == (long + short)/2 − cost.

Corrected, one strategy moved from Sharpe −8.73 to +0.34; another from −2.83 to +1.39.

All three stayed closed. The corrected figures were in-sample with intervals spanning zero, and the untouched holdout was the only thing that could settle it. Read under a pre-registration, the best of the three was falsified out of sample — losing money both before and after its training window. Overfitting, not decay.

Why it matters A wrong cost model is dangerous in both directions: it buries live strategies and it flatters dead ones. Finding the bug is half the job. Refusing to let it resurrect a corpse is the other half — and it is the half a motivated author will skip.

Staleness

The recency filter that only counted rows

The guard, as written
len(recent) > 100
Named and trusted as a staleness filter.
→
What it tested
Row count
Never a date. Frozen assets stayed selectable indefinitely.

A data migration froze 65 files. Because the filter counted rows rather than checking recency, frozen assets remained eligible for roughly 26 days of silent stale inclusion. Basket composition changed completely from one week to the next — which read as a strategy regime change and was in fact a data artifact.

A second freeze was then found inside the restarted clock: three assets stopped updating on the same date in the live schema, and one was held in a basket having frozen seven days before it was selected.

Underneath it, the finding that mattered most: one held position had 0 of 1,441 one-minute bars with non-zero volume across its entire holding period. A full-size position, booked at a return describing a trade nobody could have made.

Why it matters A filter named for a property does not necessarily test that property. And when a strategy appears to change behaviour, the first hypothesis should be that the data changed underneath it — not that the market did.

Execution cost

Three documents, one question, three different answers

Measured at the touch
25.7 bp
0.68× the assumption — reads as conservative.
→
Walking the book at size
47.8 bp
1.26× the assumption. The two disagree in direction.

Quoted spreads on that basket were 0.4–3.3bp while the true round trip was 28–78bp. The cost lived entirely in depth; a touch-based measure cannot see it.

That prompted an audit of every cost model in the research base. It found:

A standing study charged fee plus half the quoted spread and asserted the venue's worst possible round trip was 31bp. Measured by walking the book, 40 of 122 assets exceed 31bp at $10,000 per name — worst 92bp — and that is on the most liquid names available.

Its own follow-up note had already corrected it using a theoretical impact model, and overstated by ~1.8× in the other direction — as that note itself suspected it might. Measured, the truth sat between them, and the original conclusion survived at the size it was written for while failing an order of magnitude up.

Why it matters A cost model is not a convention to inherit. And the error is largest exactly where a ranking rule tends to select — because ranking rules select for something other than liquidity.

What these five have in common

Not one was found by out-thinking the author. Each was found by checking something nobody thought needed checking.

  • 01Running the same code twice, in different processes.
  • 02Running an existing validation on every row instead of one.
  • 03Testing an accounting identity that had never been written down.
  • 04Asking whether a filter tests the property it is named for.
  • 05Measuring a cost instead of inheriting a convention.

What gets checked

The standing checklist is derived from failure modes actually encountered, not from a textbook.

  1. Reproduction. Does the published number come out of the code, twice, in different processes?
  2. Survivorship. Was the universe enumerated as-of, or on today's survivors? Was any fix checked against the property it is named for?
  3. Cost model. Fee basis sourced to a schedule? Charged at book level or per position? Measured at the touch, or by walking the book at the intended size?
  4. Data integrity. Input series validated across the panel, or spot-checked? Rolling windows inspected for single-day contamination?
  5. Staleness. Are the recency filters actually recency filters?
  6. Selection. Was the tested variant chosen before or after seeing the data? Is the holdout genuinely untouched?
  7. Tradability. Could every position in the result actually have been traded, at the price used?
  8. Statistical claim. Point estimate or interval? One-sided or two? Does the interval exclude the thing being claimed?
