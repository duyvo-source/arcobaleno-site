✓ What holds up

Signal v4 really does know something about where prices go next. On six months of data it had never been tuned on, it called direction correctly 53.1% of the time, against 33.3% for guessing. Nothing in this report says the idea is worthless. We want that read first, because most of what follows takes things away.

✗ What doesn't

The +38% a year doesn't survive the checks. We found six problems, none of them in the idea itself. They are all in what the test assumed about trading costs, prices and timing. Fix them and the same signal on the same data makes +2.2% a year, too close to zero to call a profit.

The problems are worst exactly where the money was. A third of the claimed profit came from the fastest, most chaotic 1% of moments, which is where the test's assumptions were least realistic.

! The loss the client already knew about

The client told us about one live trade where the signal said buy and should have said sell. They thought it was bad luck. Our job was to find out whether it was one event or a pattern. It is a pattern: 14 moments in the test period share its shape, and in 9 of them the signal flips the wrong way once it's tested at its real speed. It will happen again. See FC-5.

FC-1

Does it repeat?

✓Passed

What we asked: if we run your code again, on a different computer, do we get the same number?

We ran it 20 times on two different machines, and again with a hidden setting changed that often reshuffles how programs order their work. The yearly return never moved by more than 0.31 points, and the hit rate was identical every time.

✓ Why this matters

This is a real credential. Most strategies that reach us fail here; our own first finding is one that did. Because the number came back the same, the other seven checks could test the method instead of chasing a moving target. Passing doesn't mean the number is right, only that it's stable. The next six sections are all mistakes in a perfectly repeatable test.

➤ The fix: nothing to fix, one thing to keep

Lock those settings in your automated tests, so the result stays repeatable as the code changes.

Effort: under an hour, once
FC-2

Are the dead ones missing?

!Minor · −1.8 points

What we asked: was the list of markets built as it looked at the time, or from the ones still around today?

✗ What was wrong

The 12 markets are the 12 busiest as measured in early 2026, after the test period ended. Two markets that were in the top 12 during 2023 have since closed, and are missing from the test. A third only opened in mid-2024, yet the test traded it for nine months before it existed.

Markets in the test12
Markets missing because they later closed2
Months traded in a market that didn't exist yet9
Effect of rebuilding the list as it was on each date−1.8 points a year

Small, and we say so. This check often produces the biggest finding. It doesn't here, because these are 12 large, busy markets rather than hundreds of small ones. We report the size either way: “we checked, and it was minor” is a result too, and it shows the large findings elsewhere were measured rather than guessed. (We have made this mistake ourselves: finding 10 on our front page.)

➤ The fix

Build the market list from the dates each market opened and closed, not from what is trading today. Then test the property itself: no market should appear before it opened or after it closed. Checking only that “the numbers changed” is how this mistake survives a fix. Reusable across every strategy you run.

Effort: about a day
FC-3

Are the costs real?

✗−6.6 points

What we asked: where did the trading-fee figure come from, and is it charged correctly?

Exchanges charge less, and sometimes even pay a small rebate (the maker rebate), when your order waits in the order book to be filled. They charge more when you trade immediately at the current price.

✗ What was wrong

The test gave every single trade the maker rebate. The client's own exchange statements show only 61% of real trades got it. And the other 39% weren't spread evenly: they bunched up on the fast, chaotic moments, because that's when a waiting order doesn't get filled and the system has to trade immediately. The cost mistake is biggest exactly where most of the profit came from.

Test assumedReal trading
Trades that got the cheaper rate100%61%
… on the fastest 1% of moments100%23%
Cost of a round trip, all tradesearned 1.0 bppaid 4.4 bps
Cost of a round trip, fastest 1% of momentsearned 1.0 bppaid 9.1 bps
➤ The fix

Use the real share of cheaper-rate trades from your exchange statements, moment by moment, instead of assuming all of them. The statements already exist, so this is a lookup, not new modelling. Re-check it after the FC-7 fix, because the two are linked.

Effort: half a day
FC-4

Is the data clean?

✗−3.5 points

The finding that surprised the client most.

What we asked: was every piece of data checked, and could one source quietly spoil the result?

Nothing was wrong with either of the two data sources. The problem is that there were two, and they weren't the same market.

✗ What was wrong

The signal read data from the exchange it actually trades on. But each call was marked right or wrong against a price chart from a charting website, which shows a blend of prices from many exchanges, not the one traded. Both are accurate. They are just different prices.

Worse: the chart stamps each 15-minute bar with its start time, and the test read it as the end time. So every call was marked against a price 15 minutes in the future.

Gap between the chart's price and the traded price
Typical0.8 bp
Worst 1% of the time11 bps
Worst 1% of the time, during the fastest moments41 bps

Re-marking every call against the real traded price, at the right time: 7.1% of “right” calls became wrong and 1.4% of “wrong” calls became right. Net, the hit rate fell by 5.7 points.

! Also found: one bad day

On 11 March 2024 the exchange's data froze for 71 minutes. The signal read the frozen picture as a strong, steady signal and made 14 calls, 12 marked as wins. Removing that window costs only 0.4 points, but it is the same kind of mistake as finding 2 on our front page, and worth guarding against.

➤ The fix: best value per hour in this report

Mark calls against the traded exchange's own prices, and correct the start-versus-end time. Do this before any other fix: until it's done, every number you use to measure the other fixes is measured against the wrong market. Separately, make the data feed raise an alarm when the picture stops changing, instead of passing it on as a strong signal.

Effort: about an hour
FC-5

Is the data current?

✗−4.1 points

The finding that explains the loss the client already knew about.

What we asked: how old is the market picture at the moment the signal makes its call?

✗ What was wrong

The test assumed the signal sees the market instantly. Live, it can't. From the client's own system logs, we measured how old its picture of the market really is:

Age of the L2 snapshot when the call is made
Usually0.24 seconds
Worst 1% of the time1.9 seconds
Worst 1% of the time, during the fastest moments6.2 seconds
Longest seen11.4 seconds

The delay is worst exactly when the market moves fastest. On the moments that carried a third of the profit, the live system was typically looking at a picture 4.4 seconds old. In a fast market, the buyers the signal “sees” have often already left. It reads a wall that is no longer there as strength.

This is what happened in the client's known loss. We didn't go looking for it; it came out of the measurements. A buy call into a move that had already happened, because the picture behind it was seconds old.

One event or a pattern? We found every moment in the three years that looked like it: a very fast move, just after one side of the order book thinned out sharply.

Moments with the same shape, 2023–202514
Calls that flip the wrong way at the real delay9 of 14
… of which the test had marked as wins9 of 9
Share of the claimed +38.0% coming from those 9 calls11.4 points

A pattern, not an accident. The known loss is these 14 moments behaving normally, and it will happen again on the next one.

➤ The fix: three steps, and the cheap one matters most

(a) A live safety switch: skip any call made on a market picture older than a set limit. This stops the next one of these reaching the market. On this data it would have skipped 9 of the 14, and none of them were calls you wanted. (b) Give the test the real delays, so research and live trading agree on what the signal can see. (c) Reduce the delay itself, the biggest job and the only one that wins those calls back.

Effort: (a) ~1 day · (b) ~2 days · (c) an infrastructure project
FC-6

Was it cherry-picked?

✗−1.9 points

−16.1 points on its own (see why the order matters), and the biggest effect on the hit rate.

What we asked: how many versions were tried, and was this one chosen before or after seeing how it did?

✗ What was wrong

The code contains 41 different versions of the strategy's settings, made over fourteen months, and a script that tests all of them on the full three years. The version that was shipped is simply the one that scored best. No data was held back to check it on.

This is not an accusation. Nobody did anything dishonest. It's the most common thing we find, and we find it in our own work (finding 7 on our front page). “Try the variations, keep the one that works” is good instinct everywhere except here. The problem is arithmetic: with 41 tries on the same data, the best one is mostly the luckiest one, and luck doesn't repeat.

How we measured it. The client's own history shows no settings were changed after 14 June 2025. So the last six months of 2025 are data no version was ever chosen on. We tested the shipped version, unchanged, on those months:

Data it was chosen onUntouched data
Calls made7,1041,180
How often it's right61.4%53.1%
Return per year (before the other fixes)+38.0%+21.9%
Rank among the 41 versions1st17th

That last row is the finding. On data it wasn't chosen on, the shipped version is ordinary: 17th of 41, in the middle of its own family.

✓ What survives

53.1% right against 33.3% for guessing, on 1,180 fresh calls, has less than a 1-in-1,000 chance of being luck. Cherry-picking inflated the number. It didn't invent it.

➤ The fix: only forwards, never backwards

You can't un-see 41 results. From now on: (a) set aside a stretch of data before the next settings change, and don't look at it until the change is final. (b) For anything you show investors, write down the pass mark before the test period starts. And don't switch to a different version because of the 17th-place ranking: that ranking is one roll of the dice too.

Effort: a policy decision, plus a day of tooling
FC-7

Could you have traded it?

✗−17.9 points

The biggest correction in this report.

What we asked: could every trade really have happened, at the price used, at that size?

First, where the profit came from. We sorted every 15-minute window by how wild the price moves were:

Windows, by how fast the market movedShare of timeShare of claimed profit
Fastest 1%0.8%34.1%
Fastest 5%4.9%61.3%
The other 95%95.1%38.7%

A third of the profit came from less than 1% of the time. Whatever the test assumed about those moments is very nearly the whole result.

✗ What was wrong

The test assumed every trade was filled, as a waiting order, at the mid-price at the moment of the call, with certainty. On a fast move that's backwards: the waiting orders that get filled are the ones the market is running away from. On the fastest 1% of moments we checked whether the assumed price was still there:

Fast moments where the price moved past the order within 1 second71.4%
Typical distance the price had moved before the order could even sit in the order book19 bps
Trades filled at a price never seen again in that window2,847 of 7,104

We re-ran the test with one change: when the price had already moved away, the trade happens immediately at the price that actually existed. The client's own exchange data has both. Everything else was left alone. +26.1% became +8.2%.

Why this is a mistake, not a matter of opinion. Whether a waiting order gets filled depends on what happens after the call. The test answered that question with information it couldn't have had yet, and picked the favourable answer every time.

➤ The fix: flip the default

Assume every trade happens immediately at the real price, unless the exchange data shows a waiting order really would have been filled. That makes the test cautious instead of optimistic. To prove the change worked: with position limits set so high they never bite, old and new tests must agree exactly.

Effort: 2–3 days, including re-running the tests
FC-8

What is really being claimed?

✗Can't rule out zero

What we asked: is the headline one exact number, or a range? And is the range wide enough to include zero?

✗ What was wrong

“+38.0% a year” was stated to one decimal place, with no range, no margin of error and no number of trades beside it. FC-6 already showed what it really was: the best of 41 tries.

So we asked what the corrected number can support. We re-sampled the corrected results 10,000 times, a week at a time, to see how far the figure could move:

Best estimateLikely range (95%)
Return per year+2.2%−5.4% to +9.9%
Return for the risk taken (Sharpe ratio)0.14−0.35 to 0.63
How often it's right (untouched data)53.1%50.2% to 56.0%

These three rows don't say the same thing:

  • The return range includes zero. On this evidence, the strategy can't be told apart from one that makes nothing.
  • The risk-adjusted range includes zero too. Same message.
  • The hit rate range is nowhere near guessing (33.3%). The signal's knowledge is proven. Its profit is not.

That gap is the real state of this strategy, and a common one: a signal that knows something, but whose edge is smaller than the cost of acting on it in real trading.

! The comparison nobody made

Beating guesswork isn't the real test. The simple alternative is: just holding the same 12 markets, equally weighted, over 2023–2025 made clearly more than +2.2% a year. It isn't exactly the same kind of trade, but none of the documents names any comparison at all, and a return with nothing to compare it to doesn't yet say anything about value. Choosing the benchmark is the client's call, and it should be written down in advance.

➤ The fix: change what you publish, not what you calculate

Quote a range, with the number of trades beside it, and name what you're comparing against. A number an investor can check is worth more than a bigger one they can't.

Effort: a decision, and an afternoon to redo the one-pager

What this report does not say

An audit that only lists damage isn't worth paying for. So, plainly:

✓ Still true after the audit
  • The idea is not disproved. 53.1% right against 33.3% for guessing, on untouched data. There is real knowledge in this signal, and none of the six corrections touched the signal itself.
  • The signal's own code had no mistakes. Every finding is in the testing around it: fills, timing, costs, the prices used for marking, the market list, and how the version was chosen.
  • FC-1 passed cleanly. That's a genuine credential; most don't.
  • No dishonesty is suggested anywhere. We have made every one of these mistakes ourselves.
  • This is not a forecast. It says what the material supplied can and can't support today. A fixed version is a different strategy and needs its own evidence.

Your plan, in order

Every fix above, ordered by how much it changes the claim for the work it takes.

Each fix is written precisely enough for your own team to do without us, on purpose. We don't carry out fixes on a strategy we have audited. An auditor who also sells the repair has a reason to make the problems look bigger, and this report is only worth anything because we don't. Nor do we tell you how to make the signal better. Making your measurement honest is our job; making the strategy better is your edge, and it should stay yours.

  1. Mark calls against the right price first (FC-4). About an hour of work, and until it's done every other number is measured against the wrong market.
  2. Assume trades happen at real prices by default (FC-7). This turns the biggest source of over-statement into a cautious assumption.
  3. Measure the live delay and feed it into the test (FC-5). This is the finding that predicts the next live loss, and the safety switch stops it.
  4. Write down the pass mark before the next settings change (FC-6). Cherry-picking can't be undone, only prevented.
  5. Redo the investor one-pager (FC-8). Whatever the corrected figure turns out to be, publish it as a range, with the number of trades and a benchmark beside it.

What we did not check

  • Anything after 31 December 2025. The material supplied ends there.
  • The live trading system's own code. We used its logs to measure delays (FC-5), but the parts that place orders, manage risk and reconcile accounts were outside this audit.
  • Counterparty, custody, margin and liquidation risks.
  • Whether the exchange data supplied is complete. We checked it against the client's own statements, which tests consistency, not independent truth.
  • Any market beyond the 12 supplied, or any call interval other than 15 minutes.
